## What do we mean by progress?

In Matthew Berman's interview with Sam Altman, the exchange around 6:51–7:04 raised a question that matters to me. The wording below follows my replay and transcription.

Berman:
> Yeah, I think that's just human nature. Like if we were comfortable with the way life was a hundred years ago, now, we'd probably be working an hour a week, but that's just not the way it is.

Altman:
> And I think it's a beautiful thing about human nature that that's not the way it is.

[Watch the exchange](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3gXLmy7DK_4&t=411s).

My response concerns the historical baseline. Looking back a century from 2026 takes us to **1926**. Whose life, work and comfort are we comparing? Who could own the result of their labor, and who faced exclusion?

The early American record makes those questions concrete. In colonial Virginia, the 1662 law made a child's enslaved or free status follow the mother's. Lineage was made a legal mechanism for passing bondage between generations. The Declaration's equality principle existed alongside a constitutional order that counted enslaved people toward representation without enfranchising them. The 1790 naturalization framework restricted eligibility to free white immigrants. These were rules governing power and opportunity, not merely private prejudices. [Records 1–4](SOURCES.md#historical-records)

Emancipation and constitutional amendments changed the law. That matters. Yet Virginia enacted a Public Assemblages Act in 1926 requiring racial separation in public venues. The Department of Veterans Affairs also documents how, in the 1920s and 1930s, Black veterans could receive only emergency admission at hospitals lacking facilities designated for them before transfer elsewhere. These examples concern public life and medical access. [Records 5–7](SOURCES.md#historical-records)

**Black Americans were explicitly targeted by the institutions in these records.** They were not the only people excluded: federal restrictions also targeted Asian immigration. The evidence identifies laws, institutions and decisions. It does not justify assigning guilt by ancestry or treating every member of a race, sex or generation as having the same power or responsibility. [Record 8](SOURCES.md#historical-records)

My point is that an account of progress must examine who shares its benefits. More production alone does not establish fair compensation, dignity, access or freedom.

## How inequality can enter an algorithm

This concern has an empirical example. In a 2019 study, Obermeyer and colleagues examined a healthcare algorithm that used spending to predict need. At the same assigned risk score, Black patients were sicker than white patients. Unequal access and spending made cost a misleading stand-in for illness. A system could predict its chosen measure while allocating help unfairly.

That study establishes a specific mechanism by which social inequality can enter an automated decision. It does not establish the motives of an interview participant or prove misconduct by an unrelated company. [Original study and scope](SOURCES.md#algorithmic-evidence)

Understanding requires connecting the rules, the measures and their consequences. Wisdom requires deciding what we should do with that understanding. My concern is whether greater capability serves people justly.
